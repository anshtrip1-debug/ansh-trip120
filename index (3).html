tolstack Monte Carlo tolerance analysis

Python, no dependencies

Worst-case stack-up assumes every part in an assembly arrives at the wrong end of its tolerance band on the same day. That almost never happens, so designers who trust it pay for tolerances they did not need. RSS gets closer, but falls apart the moment a process runs off centre.

tolstack samples each dimension from the distribution it actually has (normal, uniform or triangular, with an optional mean shift) and reports all three methods side by side. It also ranks each dimension by its share of the output variance, which points to the one tolerance worth paying to tighten.

On the bearing seat example, worst case says the design fails outright. 200,000 sampled assemblies say 125 ppm and a Cpk of 1.17, with the housing bore owning 61 percent of the variance.

ik planar inverse kinematics

C++17, single translation unit

Two solvers. One is a closed-form law of cosines solution for a two-link arm that returns both the elbow-up and elbow-down answers, and reports "out of reach" and "base dead zone" as separate failures. The other is damped least squares for any number of links, with joint limits.

The damping matters most. Near full extension the Jacobian loses rank, a plain pseudo-inverse asks for enormous joint rates, and a real arm snaps. Levenberg-Marquardt damping keeps the joint rate bounded. The solver also reports manipulability as a warning light for singularities.

Ten tests, including the one people skip: seeding each solve from the previous pose so a traced path stays continuous. The largest joint step over a 200-point ellipse is 0.015 rad.

profile jerk-limited motion profiles

C++17 core, Python plotter

Trapezoidal and seven-segment double-S profiles for a single axis. A trapezoid steps its acceleration instantly, so jerk is unbounded at four instants in every move. Each step is a broadband kick straight into the machine's first structural mode, which is why cheap gantries ring.

The jerk-limited planner solves the geometry in closed form instead of searching, including the two cases that break naive implementations: a move too short to reach top speed, and a move too short to reach peak acceleration either.

On a 400-unit move the jerk-limited profile takes 8.4 percent longer on paper and stops exciting the structure.

How I pick problems

Three rules I keep coming back to.

  1. The boundary is where things break.

    Pure mechanical work and pure software are both well covered. The failures show up where a motor meets a control loop meets a tolerance stack, so that's where I like to work.

  2. Build it, then measure it.

    A claim without a measurement is a guess. Every project should end with a number somebody could argue with.

  3. Ship to real people.

    JaamTech taught me more in a workshop room than in a deploy log. Software that never reaches anyone is still a prototype.
